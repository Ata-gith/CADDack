Can it tell when it's wrong?

A Bayesian network predicts binding affinity from the ligand's 2D graph and the 3D geometry of the binding site, and reports an uncertainty with every prediction. This page checks that uncertainty against the errors the model actually made.

What is being predicted

A protein has a pocket on its surface. A drug-like molecule — the ligand — sits in that pocket. The question is how tightly the two stick together.

Tightness is measured as a dissociation constant Kd, the ligand concentration at which half the pockets are occupied. It spans many orders of magnitude, so it is reported as pK = −log10 Kd. Higher means tighter, and one unit means ten times tighter. The molecules here run from pK 2 to 12, with a standard deviation of 1.88.

The input is a set of 3D coordinates: the ligand's atoms, plus every protein atom within 6 Å of it. A typical case is 27 ligand atoms and 140 pocket atoms. The output is a single number, plus a statement of how sure the model is about it.

The model

Two encoders read the same complex in different ways, and their outputs are concatenated and fed to a probabilistic head.

Message passing
Each atom starts as a vector of its own properties. Repeatedly, every atom's vector is updated with a learned function of its bonded neighbours' vectors. After three rounds each atom's vector reflects its local substructure. Summing them gives one vector for the molecule.
Distance convolutions
The same idea on coordinates instead of bonds. Atoms within 6 Å are treated as connected, and each message is weighted by a smooth function of the distance between them. Only distances are used, never raw positions, so the output is unchanged if the whole complex is rotated or translated.
Vina terms
Six scalars from AutoDock Vina's empirical scoring function: four shaped functions of how far each ligand–protein atom pair sits from van der Waals contact, a hydrophobic-contact count, and a hydrogen-bond count. Hand-derived physics rather than anything learned.
Probabilistic head
An ordinary neural network has one fixed number per weight. Here each weight is a Gaussian distribution, and a prediction is made by drawing a sample from each. Thirty draws give thirty slightly different answers.

Two kinds of uncertainty

The head reports a spread as well as a value, and that spread is split into two parts with different meanings.

Epistemic — the model's own doubt
The disagreement between those thirty draws. If they cluster tightly, the training data pinned this prediction down; if they scatter, it did not. In principle this shrinks as training data grows, because it reflects ignorance rather than anything about the world.
Aleatoric — irreducible noise
A second output, trained to predict how noisy this particular measurement is. The affinities come from different laboratories using different assays, and the same complex measured twice can differ by around 0.5 pK. No amount of extra data removes that. The model is told to estimate it rather than pretend it is zero.

They combine as σtotal = √(σepistemic² + σaleatoric²), which is the ± figure shown with each prediction.

Reading the numbers

Five quantities, all computed on the 740 complexes held out of training.

MAE pK units, lower is better
Mean absolute error: the average size of a miss. An MAE of 1.2 means the typical prediction is off by a factor of about 16 in binding strength. Predicting the training mean for everything scores 1.59, so that is the number to beat for the model to be doing anything at all.
Pearson r −1 to 1, higher is better
How well predictions and measurements move together as a straight line. It is unaffected by scale: a model that predicts exactly half the true value every time still scores 1. So it measures ranking — whether the model can tell a tight binder from a weak one — and ignores whether the absolute numbers are right.
R² ≤ 1, higher is better
1 − Σ(pred − true)² / Σ(true − mean)². The fraction of the variance the model accounts for. Unlike r it does punish scale and offset errors, so r can be high while R² is poor. Zero means no better than predicting the mean; negative means worse.
Coverage target 68% and 95%
How often the measurement actually falls inside the model's stated ±1σ and ±2σ. For a well-calibrated Gaussian these should be 68% and 95%. Below means the error bars are too narrow — overconfident. Above means too wide.
Spearman ρ −1 to 1, higher is better
Correlation between the model's stated uncertainty and the size of the error it actually made, computed on ranks rather than values. This is the question coverage cannot answer: not "are the error bars the right width on average" but "do they get wider on the predictions that turn out wrong". Zero means they carry no per-prediction information.

What would improve this

A random forest on fifteen scalar descriptors — molecular weight, lipophilicity, atom counts, and the six Vina terms — reaches r 0.636 and MAE 1.154 on this same split. The network here is level on ranking and behind on accuracy, so it is not yet earning its extra complexity. That comparison, rather than published leaderboards, is what the next change should be measured against.

1. Interaction features, not just atom features
The Vina terms helped because they describe pairs of atoms across the interface, which is what binding is. The learned encoders describe atoms, and have to discover pairwise structure themselves from 2,959 examples. Counting contacts binned by distance and atom-type pair — the approach behind the best published scoring models — gives that structure directly instead of hoping it is learned.
2. Direction, not just distance
The geometry encoder uses only the distance between atoms, which makes it correctly indifferent to rotation but blind to angle. Hydrogen bonds are strongly directional — a donor pointing at an acceptor is worth far more than one beside it — and that distinction is currently invisible.
3. Chemistry the pocket is missing
Only element and bonded-neighbour counts reach the model. Partial charges, explicit hydrogens and metal ions are absent; cofactors and metals are in fact discarded during preprocessing, so a zinc-dependent enzyme arrives without its zinc. Electrostatics is the largest single term in most binding models and is entirely missing here.
4. More data, and the right kind
2,959 training complexes against roughly 420,000 parameters. The full PDBbind general set is about four times larger at lower structural quality, and pretraining on it before fine-tuning on the refined subset is the standard remedy. Worth noting that simply using more data has already been tried here without moving the ranking, which is why it sits below the representation changes.
5. Stop the variance absorbing the error
Predictions span only 0.43 to 0.76 of the measured spread depending on the run. With a loss that lets the model widen its own error bars, hedging toward the mean is a cheap way to reduce it. Weighting the loss so that cannot pay, or calibrating the output scale against held-out data, would convert existing ranking ability into accuracy without changing the architecture.

The honest order is 1, 2, 3 before 4 and 5. Several uncertainty mechanisms have been tried here — a Bayesian posterior, dropout sampling, and an ensemble of five independently trained networks — and none produced an error signal better than measuring how similar the ligand is to the training set. That consistently points at what the model can represent rather than at how it expresses doubt.

Data and caveats

Trained on the PDBbind v2015 refined set: 3,699 protein–ligand complexes with crystal structures and measured affinities. The split holds out whole Bemis–Murcko scaffolds — the ring skeleton of a molecule — so the test ligands are structurally novel rather than close relatives of the training ones. That is harder than the usual random split, and the numbers are lower because of it.

Affinity benchmarks built on this data are known to overlap heavily between train and test, which inflates published results. The scaffold split here reduces that risk without eliminating it. The pocket stops at 6 Å, so the model never sees the wider protein, and one held-out split is one sample.
