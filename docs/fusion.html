Binding affinity from structure

A random forest predicts how tightly a molecule binds a protein pocket, from 1,137 numbers describing the two. A neural network trained on the same data is shown alongside it.

Loading predictions…

These predictions were computed ahead of time, not in your browser. This model needs a protein–ligand complex with 3D coordinates, which you cannot type into a box, and its Bayesian head needs Monte Carlo sampling over weight posteriors. The solubility page does run its model live; this one explores results from a held-out test set.

The task

A ligand sits in a pocket on a protein's surface. How tightly it holds is a dissociation constant, reported as pK = −log10 Kd: higher is tighter, one unit is ten times tighter. These complexes span pK 2 to 12, with a standard deviation of 1.88.

Input: the ligand's atoms and every protein atom within 8 Å, typically 27 and 140. Output: one number.

The model

1,137 numbers per complex, fed to 300 decision trees whose predictions are averaged.

Vina terms
Six numbers from AutoDock Vina's scoring function, each a sum over ligand–protein atom pairs of a function of their separation: four shaped energy terms, a hydrophobic-contact count, a hydrogen-bond count.
Contact counts
Every ligand–protein atom pair sorted into a bin by element pair (C, N, O, sulfur-and-halogens, other) and separation (under 3.5 Å, then 4.5, 6, 8 Å). Five classes squared by four shells is 100 counts, divided by ligand atom count.
Where the accuracy comes from
The groups were added in order and each was measured: descriptors alone give r 0.589, adding the Vina terms gives 0.636, adding the contact counts gives 0.696. Both of the groups that helped describe pairs of atoms facing each other, which is what binding is.

Reading the numbers

All computed on 740 complexes held out of training.

pK the thing being predicted
Binding strength is measured as a dissociation constant Kd: the concentration of drug at which half the protein's binding sites are occupied. A tight binder works at low concentration, so smaller means better, and real values run from millimolar down to picomolar — nine orders of magnitude. Taking the negative base-10 logarithm turns that into a comfortable linear scale: pK = −log10 Kd, with Kd in moles per litre. Now bigger means tighter, and each +1 is a tenfold improvement. 1 mM is pK 3, 1 µM is pK 6, 1 nM is pK 9. This dataset runs from about 2 to 12 and averages 6.3; a marketed drug is usually 8 or better. The same arithmetic applies to the errors: being off by 1.0 pK means being wrong about the concentration by a factor of 10.
MAE pK, lower better
Average size of a miss. 1.07 means the typical prediction is off by a factor of about 12 in binding strength. Predicting the training mean scores 1.59.
Pearson r −1 to 1, higher better
How well predictions and measurements line up. Unaffected by scale: halving every prediction still scores 1. It measures ranking, not accuracy.
R² ≤ 1, higher better
1 − Σ(pred − true)² / Σ(true − mean)². Fraction of variance accounted for. Unlike r it punishes scale and offset, so r can be high while R² is poor. Zero means no better than predicting the mean.
Coverage target 68% and 95%
How often the measurement falls inside the stated ±1σ and ±2σ. Below target means the error bars are too narrow.
Spearman ρ −1 to 1, higher better
Rank correlation between a model's stated uncertainty and the error it made. Coverage asks whether the error bars are the right width on average; ρ asks whether they widen on the predictions that turn out wrong.

Network prediction

A neural network was trained on the same complexes and the same split. It is kept here because it fails in an instructive way, marked in red below.

Tower
One encoder: a stack of layers turning a view of the complex into a fixed-length vector of 128 numbers. This network has two, reading different things, joined end to end before the final layers.
Ligand tower
Reads the molecule as a graph of atoms and bonds, no coordinates. Each atom starts as a vector of its properties; three rounds of updating each atom from its bonded neighbours leave every vector describing a local substructure. Summing them gives the molecule's vector.
Geometry tower
Reads the 3D coordinates of ligand and pocket together. Atoms within 6 A count as connected, and each message between them is weighted by a smooth function of their separation. Distances only, never positions, so rotating the complex changes nothing.
Bayesian head
The layers after the towers hold a Gaussian distribution per weight rather than a single value. Each prediction samples every weight, so thirty draws give thirty slightly different answers. Only the head works this way here: the 324,000 weights in the two towers are fixed numbers, and 197,000 in the head are distributions. The term that pulls those distributions toward the prior is also scaled down by a factor of 100 during training, which improved accuracy but makes the result narrower than a posterior proper.

Why it fails

Binding is a property of pairs: which ligand atom faces which pocket atom, and how far apart. The geometry tower is the only part that sees both molecules, and it does learn a vector for each atom describing that atom's surroundings. Then it adds the vectors up.

Addition forgets who was next to whom. After the sum, a carbon sitting 3 Å from a backbone oxygen and a carbon sitting 7 Å from nothing are both just carbons in the total. What survives is close to a count of atoms by type, which is why a model given nothing but counts matches this one.

The towers are reproducible by nine trivial numbers
A random forest on nine scalars — molecular weight, atom counts and the like, none of which describe the interface — reaches r 0.589 against the network's 0.644. Pocket atom count on its own accounts for R² 0.416. Whatever the 420,000 tower parameters compute, a handful of counts stands in for most of it.
It is not the features either
Handed the same 113 structural features alongside its towers, the network reached r 0.626 against the forest's 0.696 on a matched split: closer, still behind. At 2,959 training complexes bagged trees on well-built features are hard to beat, and the network also had to discover pairwise structure from those examples while the forest was handed it.
More data is not the missing piece
The obvious reply is that 2,959 complexes is simply too few. Training the forest on 3.7× as many — PDBbind's general set on top, with any ligand sharing a scaffold with the test set removed — moved r by +0.005 over five splits, inside the noise. It did more than halve the test ligands with no close training analogue, so the extra data buys reach rather than accuracy. The forest above ships trained that way. This is measured on the forest, not the network, so it does not settle the question for the network; it does show the dataset is not obviously starved.
Handing the pairs over directly is what worked
The contact counts in the forest are exactly the information the sum throws away: every ligand–protein atom pair, sorted by element pair and separation. Adding them lifted r from 0.636 to 0.696, the largest single effect measured here. The lesson is not that networks cannot do this — it is that pooling has to preserve what the problem is about.
The forest also runs in a browser
Trees serialise and can be walked in JavaScript, which is how the panel above scores an uploaded pose. The network builds its graph inside the forward pass and cannot be exported that way.

The forest's uncertainty

Every prediction carries a spread. The forest's 300 trees each see a different bootstrap sample of the training complexes and a different subset of features at each branch, so they disagree; the standard deviation across their 300 answers is the spread. Nothing trains it — it is a by-product of how the forest is built.

Two things could warn that a prediction is unreliable: that disagreement, and the Tanimoto distance from the ligand to the nearest one in training, meaning the fraction of structural fingerprint bits the two fail to share. Ranked against the errors actually made:

The network's Bayesian version of this does not work
The network holds a Gaussian distribution over its head weights and reports the scatter across thirty samples as epistemic uncertainty. That term ranks errors at rho −0.08 ± 0.04 across five training runs: no signal, and the wrong sign. Sampling both towers as well, which lifts the stochastic share of the weights from 21% to 97%, left it at −0.07 ± 0.05. Gaussian weight noise spreads a prediction in proportion to the size of its input, not to how far that input sits from the training data, and nothing in the objective widens the posterior where data is absent. The forest needs no such machinery: its trees disagree because they genuinely saw different data, and they disagree most where there was least of it.

Data and caveats

PDBbind v2015: crystal structures with measured affinities. The held-out 740 complexes come from the quality-filtered refined set of 3,699, split on whole Bemis–Murcko scaffolds — a molecule's ring skeleton — so test ligands are structurally new, not close relatives of training ones. Harder than a random split, and the numbers are lower for it.

Training adds PDBbind's larger general set on top, taking it from 2,959 complexes to 10,805, after dropping any general ligand sharing a scaffold with the test set. That barely moves accuracy — r +0.005 over five splits, inside the noise — but it more than halves the test ligands with no close training analogue, from 184 to 85. General-set affinities come from more varied assays and lower-resolution structures, so this trades label quality for chemical breadth. The test set stays refined-only, so the evaluation above is unaffected.

Three caveats. Affinity benchmarks on PDBbind overlap heavily between train and test, which inflates published results; the scaffold split reduces that without eliminating it. Scaffold splitting separates ligands but not proteins, so the same pocket can appear on both sides — holding out whole proteins as well costs 0.03 in r, measured against a control that drops the same amount of training data at random, so the numbers here are optimistic by about that much. And the features stop at 8 Å, so the wider protein is never seen.
